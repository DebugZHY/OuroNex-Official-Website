SODA is the numerical workflow we describe as sitting at the centre of SIDA's analysis capability. It is research-driven: second-order direct analysis methods, implemented as software, with the assumptions of the method written down where a reviewer can find them.

That description is deliberate. Numerical work is never assumption-free. A method chooses how to represent stiffness, how to treat second-order effects, when a step has converged and what to do when it has not. Those choices can be documented, or they can be hidden behind a result. We aim for the first.

## Against the black box

A black box is not a method; it is a description of what the user cannot see. Tools of that kind may produce numbers, but they leave the engineer unable to check the reasoning, and therefore unable to take real responsibility for it. We do not accept that description for SODA. Established numerical methods, explicit assumptions and engineering review are the three parts of the claim.

> A result you cannot interrogate is not an engineering result. It is an assertion.

Explicit means checkable. Each assumption should be stated in terms a reviewer can test: what was idealised, what was neglected, and what range the method is intended for. Where our research is still under way, we say so, and we describe the module as a direction rather than a finished product. The same qualification applies to SIDA, the concept it belongs to: an orchestration of specialist agents over engineering context, with every output returning to a qualified engineer.

In scoped engagements, that posture shapes what we offer. The module is designed to embed in SIDA and extend for customized developments, so firm-specific methods can join the same reviewed workflow. No performance claim is implied. The point is not that software replaces judgement. The point is that judgement should have something it can examine.

Nothing here is a promise about results. It is a position on how numerical work should be presented: methods named, assumptions written, review retained. If a tool cannot be described that way, we do not think it is ready to carry engineering weight.
